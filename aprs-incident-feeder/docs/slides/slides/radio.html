GETTING IT ON THE AIR

A script puts the objects near you on 144.39

ASKS

Every two minutes

It asks the feeder for the objects within a chosen distance of your station.

PICKS

The few that matter

Medium and up, ten objects at most, plus a bulletin for anything critical.

SENDS

Through your KISS TNC

Direwolf or a hardware TNC, under your own callsign, in third-party form.

python3 aprs_radio.py --callsign W3XYZ-10 --lat 40.00 --lon -75.00

It needs only Python and prints what it would send until you add --transmit. Add --auto to carry everything nearby, slowly, most important first. Install guide: aprs-incidents.phlm.sh/docs

Today the objects reach APRS-IS only, so you need an internet-connected client. This script is how a station in the area can carry them on RF. Third-party form keeps the feeder as the owner of the object and stops igates sending the packet back to the internet a second time. Within one 25 mile radius there are about 130 objects, which is why the script starts small. The command shown asks this feeder and talks to Direwolf on the same machine, which are the defaults; --server names another feeder, --kiss-tcp another TNC address, and a TNC on a serial port uses --kiss-serial with its device name. The script is in the aprs-incident-rf-bridge folder of the project on GitHub. Its installer sets it up as a service that starts at boot: it asks for your callsign, your position and a radius, and leaves transmitting off until you turn it on. It has keyed a real radio through Direwolf: two objects and their kills went out on 144.39. What is still unconfirmed is a second station decoding them. Bulletins for critical objects in your area are transmitted too, ahead of the objects. With --auto the script takes every object in its radius, whatever the level, and sends only two or three packets per poll from a queue: kills, then bulletins, then objects by importance, so a new fire jumps ahead of roadwork and the low ones go out only when nothing more important is waiting.
